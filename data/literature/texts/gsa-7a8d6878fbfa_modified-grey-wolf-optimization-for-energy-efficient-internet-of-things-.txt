Modified grey wolf optimization for energy-efficient internet of things task scheduling in fog computing Deafallah Alsadie1 & Musleh Alsulami2 Fog-cloud computing has emerged as a transformative paradigm for managing the growing demands of Internet of Things (IoT) applications, where efficient task scheduling is crucial for optimizing system performance. However, existing task scheduling methods often struggle to balance makespan minimization and energy efficiency in dynamic and resource-constrained fog-cloud environments. Addressing this gap, this paper introduces a novel Task Scheduling algorithm based on a modified Grey Wolf Optimization approach (TS-GWO), tailored specifically for IoT requests in fog-cloud systems. The proposed TS-GWO incorporates innovative operators to enhance exploration and exploitation capabilities, enabling the identification of optimal scheduling solutions. Extensive evaluations using both synthetic and real-world datasets, such as NASA Ames iPSC and HPC2N workloads, demonstrate the superior performance of TS-GWO over established metaheuristic methods. Notably, TS-GWO achieves improvements in makespan by up to 46.15% and reductions in energy consumption by up to 28.57%. These results highlight the potential of TS-GWO to effectively address task scheduling challenges in fog-cloud environments, paving the way for its application in broader optimization tasks. Keywords Grey wolf optimizer (GWO), Fog-cloud computing, Task scheduling, Internet of things (IoT), Metaheuristic algorithm In the contemporary landscape, the expansion of telecommunication networks profoundly influences the ongoing Internet of Things (IoT) revolution, which continues to gain momentum. Terminal devices assume a pivotal role in environmental sensing and data generation. However, due to their inherent limitations, these devices often resort to transmitting data to the cloud for storage, processing, analysis, and decision-making, owing to its robust computational and storage capabilities. Despite the ubiquitous accessibility of cloud computing resources, it falls short in addressing the latency requirements of latency-sensitive IoT applications1. This underscores the complementary role of fog computing vis-a-vis the cloud, rather than serving as a substitute. The collaboration between cloud and fog computing is crucial to accommodate the diverse task lengths and computational demands. The proliferation of edge-cloud computing has led to a surge in user requests, which vary between cloud and fog nodes based on factors like task length, task sensitivity, and performance metrics such as delay, makespan, cost, and energy consumption2. However, the unique characteristics of fog and cloud computing, coupled with the unpredictable nature of user requests and resource constraints, render task scheduling optimization a complex and indispensable topic worthy of discussion. Task scheduling in fog-cloud computing systems plays a crucial role in optimizing performance and efficiently allocating computational resources1,3,4. The emergence of fog computing, driven by the proliferation of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, offers decentralized computation by extending the cloud to the edge of the network. This proximity to IoT devices reduces latency and enhances response times compared to traditional cloud computing architectures4,5. Despite the benefits of fog computing, efficient task scheduling remains challenging due to factors such as unbalanced load distribution, dynamic network conditions, and varying resource availability6,7. These challenges can lead to delays, resource underutilization, and inefficient energy consumption, underscoring the importance of developing practical task-scheduling algorithms. To address these challenges, researchers have proposed various intelligent optimization techniques, including evolutionary algorithms7, ant colony optimization8, Q-learning2, game theory 9, and multi-objective evolutionary algorithms 10. Related work Task scheduling in fog-cloud computing systems is critical due to issues like unbalanced load and excessive resource utilization, leading to delays and overloads in cloud data centers 6. Fog computing addresses these challenges by decentralizing processing, yet efficient task scheduling remains essential for optimal resource allocation. In IoT environments, where user demands are high, and fog computing platforms offer superior performance compared to cloud platforms, optimizing task allocation becomes crucial. To tackle the NP-hard complexity of multi-task scheduling, various intelligent optimization techniques have been proposed. Nguyen et al.7 introduced an evolutionary algorithm for IoT task scheduling in cloud-fog systems, aiming to balance time cost and task arrangement efficiently. Boveiri et al.8 proposed an enhanced ant colony optimization-based approach to optimize task order in multiprocessor environments. Tong et al. 2 developed a Q-learning task scheduling algorithm by combining neural network and Q-learning techniques to address IoT task scheduling in cloud systems. Y ang et al.9 introduced a strategy based on game theory to improve the reliability of IoT task scheduling in cloud environments. Moreover, in a separate study, they presented a multi-objective evolutionary algorithm aimed at optimizing both performance time and resource allocation in fog computing environments in10. Other approaches include 5, who used visualization methods to optimize energy cost and delay in fog computing, and14, who utilized the moth-flame algorithm to optimize task scheduling in fog nodes for improved quality of service. Abualigah et al. 15 introduced an intelligent task scheduling method based on the GWO to optimize time cost and resource allocation in cloud computing. Zeng et al.16 introduced a scheduling technique tailored for fog computing systems that cater to embedded systems. The primary aim was to reduce task execution time, thereby boosting user engagement. These studies collectively contribute to addressing the challenges of task scheduling in fog-cloud environments and supporting efficient resource utilization. Various meta-heuristic algorithms face challenges such as randomness issues, limited global search capabilities, and convergence problems, often resulting in suboptimal solutions17. Researchers have turned to the GWO due to its effectiveness in overcoming these limitations 18. Unlike Particle Swarm Optimization (PSO), which relies on individual particles to select the best solution, GWO uses the three best solutions to prevent convergence to local optima19. Moreover, GWO requires fewer parameters compared to Genetic Algorithms (GA), reducing complexity, computation time, and energy consumption 19. GWO’s dynamic nature makes it superior to Ant Colony Optimization (ACO), which does not account for the dynamic characteristics of computing resources. Introduced as a meta-heuristic approach by 20, GWO leverages swarm intelligence, mimicking the hierarchical structure and social interactions of grey wolves during hunting. Its benefits include superior convergence, minimal parameterization, and ease of implementation21. GWO has found applications in various domains such as optimization, classification, economic modeling, power dispatch, and vehicle routing 22. With the growing interest in Multi-Objective Problems (MOP), particularly in cloud-fog computing, researchers aim to balance delay and energy consumption 23. Fog node execution reduces delay but increases energy consumption, while cloud execution reduces user energy consumption but increases delay. Finding a tradeoff strategy between delay and energy is crucial in cloud-fog environments, where task scheduling plays a vital role in processing IoT applications. System model In a fog-cloud computing environment, the architecture is designed to handle the computational demands of IoT devices efficiently. It comprises fog nodes situated closer to the edge of the network, cloud servers located in centralized data centers, and interconnected IoT devices, as depicted in Fig. 1. • Fog nodes: Fog nodes are intermediary computing devices deployed at the network edge, closer to the IoT devices. These nodes serve to preprocess and filter the data generated by IoT devices before transmitting it to the cloud. Fog nodes are equipped with moderate computational resources and storage capabilities, enabling them to execute tasks locally and reduce latency in data processing. • Cloud servers: Cloud servers form the backbone of the fog-cloud architecture, providing vast computational resources and storage capacity. These servers are typically located in centralized data centers and are responsible for handling large-scale data processing tasks that cannot be efficiently executed at the edge. Cloud servers facilitate scalability and flexibility in managing diverse workloads from numerous IoT devices. • IoT devices: IoT devices, ranging from sensors and actuators to smartphones and wearables, generate a continuous stream of data. These devices are interconnected with fog nodes and cloud servers to transmit data for processing and analysis. The data generated by IoT devices may vary in volume, velocity, and variety, necessitating adaptive and efficient data processing mechanisms. • Communication infrastructure: The communication infrastructure in a fog-cloud environment enables seamless connectivity between IoT devices, fog nodes, and cloud servers. GWO algorithm fundamentals The Grey Wolf Algorithm (GWO) is a nature-inspired optimization technique that emulates the hunting behavior of grey wolves1,19,20. In GWO, candidate solutions are depicted as members of a wolf pack, and their positions are iteratively adjusted to enhance a given objective function. The algorithm leverages the hierarchical structure and social dynamics within wolf packs, with alpha, beta, and delta wolves taking leadership roles. GWO involves six key steps: initialization of wolf positions, selection of leader wolves based on fitness, updating positions using specialized equations reflecting different hunting strategies, ensuring boundary constraints, evaluating fitness at new positions, and updating leader wolves accordingly. This iterative process continues until termination criteria, such as convergence or a maximum number of iterations, are met. Overall, GWO effectively explores and exploits the search space to optimize the objective function. Grey Wolf Algorithm depicted in Algorithm 1 and explained in the following text. Algorithm 1. Algorithm 2. The proposed task scheduling with grey wolf optimization (TS-GWO). Initialization Representation of solutions: Each solution represents a mapping of tasks ( T ) to computing nodes, comprising fog nodes ( F ) and cloud nodes ( C). A binary decision variable xij is used to indicate whether a task ti is assigned to a node nj . Fog and cloud nodes are differentiated as follows: • Fog nodes ( F ): Characterized by lower computational capacity and energy consumption but provide low latency. • Cloud nodes (C): Offer higher computational capacity but consume more energy and have higher latency. Population initialization: The algorithm initializes a population of N grey wolves randomly within the solution space, where each wolf represents a unique task-to-node assignment. Fitness evaluation: The objective function F (x), defined as: F (x)= α · Z + β · E, (1) is used to evaluate the fitness of each solution. Here, Z represents the makespan, E denotes the energy consumption, and α and β are weighting factors. The best-performing wolves are identified as alpha (α), beta (β ), and delta (δ), which guide the optimization process. Iterative optimization The TS-GWO algorithm iteratively balances exploration (discovering new task assignments) and exploitation (refining promising assignments). Exploration phase Purpose: The exploration phase ensures a diverse search of the solution space to prevent premature convergence. Mechanism: Each grey wolf ’s position is updated using a random exploration vector ∆exp: x i(t+ 1) = x i(t)+∆ exp, (2) where x i(t) and x i(t+ 1) represent the current and updated positions of the i-th grey wolf, respectively. This operation introduces randomness in task assignments, allowing the algorithm to explore untested configurations of fog and cloud nodes. Exploitation phase Purpose: The exploitation phase refines the search around promising solutions identified by the alpha, beta, and delta wolves. Mechanism: Each grey wolf adjusts its position toward the alpha, beta, and delta wolves: x i(t+ 1) = x i(t)+∆ exp, (3) where the adjustment guides the wolf ’ s position toward regions of the solution space with higher fitness values. Position update The position update equation for each grey wolf is given by: xi(t+ 1) = xi(t)− A ·D, (4) where A is the amplitude coefficient controlling the intensity of adjustments, and D is the distance vector between the wolf ’ s current position and the target position. These parameters dynamically adapt as the algorithm progresses, balancing exploration and exploitation. Node-specific assignments • Fog node assignments: Tasks requiring low latency are prioritized for assignment to fog nodes, constrained by their limited capacity (Cfog j ) and energy consumption (Efog j ). • Cloud node assignments: Computationally intensive tasks or those exceeding fog node capacity are assigned to cloud nodes, leveraging their higher capacity (Ccloud j ) despite increased energy consumption (Ecloud j ). Fitness evaluation and solution update After each position update, the fitness of all grey wolves is reevaluated. The best-performing wolves are reassigned as the alpha, beta, and delta wolves, ensuring the solution set evolves toward better task-to-node assignments. Termination and output The algorithm continues until a termination condition is met, such as: • Maximum number of iterations (Tmax) reached. • Convergence of the solution set to a stable fitness value. The best solution ( α) is returned, providing the optimal task-to-node mapping that minimizes the combined objectives of makespan and energy consumption. Complexity analysis The computational complexity of the proposed TS-GWO algorithm is determined by analyzing its key operations. During initialization, N grey wolves are randomly positioned in the solution space, with a complexity of O(N · nT ). Fitness evaluation, which involves calculating makespan and energy consumption for nT tasks and nM nodes, contributes O(N · nT · nM ) per iteration. Updating the alpha, beta, and delta wolves requires O(N log N ), while position updates for all wolves add O(N · nT ) per iteration. These steps are repeated for Tmax iterations, resulting in a total computational complexity of: O (Tmax · N · nT · nM ). This indicates that the runtime of TS-GWO scales with the number of iterations, wolves, tasks, and nodes, reflecting its computational demands in large-scale fog-cloud environments. Key features and advantages • Dynamic resource allocation: Efficiently balances latency-sensitive tasks on fog nodes and computationally intensive tasks on cloud nodes. • Objective balancing: Simultaneously minimizes makespan and energy consumption. • Adaptive search: Dynamically balances exploration and exploitation through parameter adjustments. The improvement in makespan and other parameters in our proposed TS-GWO approach can be attributed to its enhanced ability to balance exploration and exploitation through the modified GWO algorithm. The new operators in TS-GWO allow for better search space exploitation, resulting in more optimal task-to-node assignments. This leads to reduced makespan and improved energy efficiency, particularly under the dynamic conditions of fog-cloud environments where task and resource distributions can vary. It can be noted that TS-GWO has shown promising results across a variety of datasets, including synthetic and real-world scenarios like NASA Ames iPSC and HPC2N workloads. However, while it performs well in many cases, the approach is particularly suited to environments where the task scheduling needs to consider both makespan and energy consumption as primary objectives. For environments with highly unpredictable workloads or significantly larger-scale systems, further optimization or adjustments to the algorithm may be necessary. However, TS-GWO may face challenges in extremely large-scale scenarios with complex interdependencies between tasks, where the computational cost of fitness evaluations may become prohibitive. Furthermore, the algorithm’s performance could be sensitive to the specific parameter tuning, which may require domain-specific adjustments in some cases. We plan to address these limitations in future work by exploring multi-objective optimization and hybrid approaches that can better handle such complex environments. Through extensive experimentation and comparative analysis, we have demonstrated the effectiveness of TS-GWO in minimizing makespan and optimizing energy consumption compared to existing scheduling algorithms. Furthermore, extending TS-GWO to address multi-objective optimization, particularly in the context of IoT applications, is a promising direction. Despite its promising results, there are limitations to the current TS-GWO implementation. Scalability with larger systems remains a challenge, as the algorithm may experience performance degradation when applied to very large-scale distributed environments. Furthermore, the algorithm’s computational overhead, which includes the complexity of its iterative process and fitness evaluations, could limit its applicability for real-time task scheduling in time-sensitive applications.
